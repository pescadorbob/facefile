# Vision

You can use a memory palace to remember people's names in a way that aligns tightly with the learning principles in Make It Stick—especially **elaboration**, **retrieval practice**, and **errorful encoding**. Here's a practical, realistic way to do it (not a caricatured "mnemonic trick").

## The core idea

Names are hard to remember because they're **arbitrary**. A memory palace works by:

1. Turning a name into something **concrete and imageable**
2. Binding it to a **stable location you already know**
3. Forcing **active retrieval**, not passive exposure

## Step 1: Choose the *right* kind of palace

For names, your palace should be **socially organized**, not random.

**Good options:**

- Your home, where each room represents a category (work, neighbors, friends)
- Your office, where each desk/room holds one colleague
- A standard meeting room, reused across events with fixed loci (door, table, projector, chairs)

**Make It Stick principle:** Build on existing mental structures to reduce cognitive load.

You don't need dozens of locations. **5-10 loci is plenty for most contexts.**

## Step 2: Place the person, not just the name

When you meet someone:

- Picture their face or defining feature
- Place them at a specific locus
- Then attach the name image to them

This mirrors real recall: you usually see the person first, then need the name.

## Step 3: Turn the name into an image (quickly)

You are not trying to be perfect—just distinctive enough to retrieve later.

**Techniques that work:**

- **Sound-alikes**
  - "Ben" → a bent beam
  - "Lisa" → a laser

- **Meaning-based images**
  - "Baker" → actually baking
  - "King" → a crown

- **Split long names**
  - "Anderson" → Ander (antler) + son (a kid)

**Don't worry if the image feels silly—bizarreness improves recall.**

## Step 4: Create an interaction (this part matters)

The *Make It Stick* insight here is **elaboration**.

Make the image:

- Interact with the person
- Interact with the location
- Include motion or emotion

**Example:**

You meet Sarah.
- Image: A sari (sounds like Sarah) wrapped around her arm.
- Location: The conference room door.
- Interaction: She's trying to open the door but the sari keeps catching.

**Static images fade. Interactions stick.**

## Step 5: Do immediate retrieval (non-negotiable)

**Within 10-60 seconds:**

- Mentally walk to the locus
- Retrieve the image
- Say the name out loud or silently

**Later:**

- Greet them again using their name
- Write the name from memory
- Briefly visualize the image once more

**Make It Stick principle:** Retrieval strengthens memory more than re-exposure.

**If you skip this step, the palace won't hold.**

## Step 6: Reuse structured palaces for repeated contexts

Instead of creating endless palaces:

- Reuse the same meeting-room palace every weekly meeting
- Reuse your office palace for coworkers
- Retire images when people leave your environment

This creates predictability, which lowers effort without reducing effectiveness.

## Step 7: Repair failures productively

If you forget:

- Don't mentally say "I'm bad with names"
- Reconstruct the palace
- Ask for the name again and intentionally rebuild the image

Errors followed by correction produce stronger learning than flawless performance.

## A realistic example (work setting)

You attend a meeting with 4 new people

| Locus | Person | Name Image |
|-------|--------|-----------|
| Door | Mark | A marker writing on his jacket |
| Table | Nina | A knee (sounds like knee-na) tapping |
| Screen | Paul | A pole he's leaning against |
| Window | Rachel | A rake pulling leaves |

**After the meeting:**

- Walk the room mentally
- Recall each image → name
- Use at least one name in conversation afterward

## What this does better than "just repeating names"

- Forces **active meaning-making**
- Uses **spatial memory**, which is evolutionarily strong
- Builds in **retrieval practice**
- Makes **forgetting diagnosable**, not random

That's exactly why it fits *Make It Stick* rather than contradicting it.
